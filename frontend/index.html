How to spot a moving object
  1. Quick way: press Find movers under the player — it lists the dots that changed position.
  2. Or by eye: press Play — images from different dates flash one after another.
  3. Most stars stay put. Look for a dot that jumps or drifts.
  4. A dot that only gets brighter/fainter in place is probably a variable star.
  5. Slow down the speed or step frame by frame with ← →.
  6. Too faint? Try Difference (still stars vanish) or Trail (a mover leaves a line of dots).
  7. Found something? Click it to see its spectrum and light curve, and check the sky map: is it a known object?

          3. Measure a source

          Click a dot in the image. We measure its brightness in every frame: spectrum = brightness vs. wavelength (one colour per survey pass), light curve = brightness vs. date (one colour per detector).

          Flux = background-subtracted sum in a 2-pixel aperture, in image units (not calibrated). A moving object is only inside the circle on the dates when it was there – that is expected, not an error.

          About the project

          Spot what moves in the infrared sky

          SPHEREx Blink lets you pick a spot on the sky and flip through infrared images of it taken on different dates. Stars stay put. Things that move or change brightness stand out.

          1. Pick a targetType coordinates or choose one of the five test objects.
          2. Blink and compareFlip through the dates, or switch to Difference, Stack and Trail.
          3. MeasureClick a source to see its spectrum and light curve.
          4. Check what is knownOpen the sky map to see catalogued objects around it.

          Why look for movers?

          Nearby brown dwarfs, fast stars, asteroids and comets reveal themselves by changing position between observations. The same blinking method found Pluto in 1930, and it is how astronomers search for undiscovered worlds such as the hypothetical “Planet X”.

          About the data

          This prototype runs on synthetic data that imitates SPHEREx observations, so every image on this page is simulated. The real SPHEREx images are public at NASA/IPAC IRSA; with the backend connected, this tool runs on them, and the real-data results page shows what it did (numbers and charts only, no images).

          The telescope

          SPHEREx maps the whole sky in 102 colours

          SPHEREx is a NASA space telescope that surveys the entire sky in near-infrared light. Instead of one picture, it records 102 different colours of every patch of sky, from 0.75 to 5 micrometres, and it maps the sky again about every six months.

          • 102colours (wavelength bands)
          • 0.75–5 µmnear-infrared range
          • 6detectors, working in pairs
          • ~6″per pixel
          • 4all-sky surveys in about 2 years

          How it sees colour

          Each detector sits behind a filter whose colour changes gradually across it. A source lands on a different spot in each exposure, so it is measured at a different wavelength every time, and its spectrum is assembled from many exposures. That is why this tool shows the wavelength of every frame.

          Why it helps find movers

          Because the sky is mapped again and again, the same spot is imaged on different dates, which is exactly what blinking needs. Cold objects such as brown dwarfs tend to be brightest at the long-wavelength end, so detectors D5 and D6 are the best place to look for them.

          What else it studies

          Beyond moving objects, SPHEREx studies how the universe began (cosmic inflation), how galaxies formed and grew, and where water and other ices are found in our galaxy.
